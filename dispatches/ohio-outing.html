By Order of the Committee · Duly Entered into the Great Ledger

The Society
Goes South

Six years. Two invitations. Not one Kavanagh. The Society has concluded that if the Ohio men will not drive, then somebody shall have to drive to them — and upon the eighteenth of October, somebody will.

Filed by Mr. Barnaby Prescott · Saturday, the Eighteenth of October, MMXXVI

Six years. Two formal invitations. Not one Kavanagh. I have been asked to file on the Ohio matter and I intend to do it properly, which means putting that at the top where everyone can see it.

The Great Northern Open went off on the eleventh of July at Pine Valley. Two seats which the membership had every reason to expect would be occupied were, at the appointed hour, conspicuously vacant. Thomas and John Kavanagh, brothers, of Columbus in the State of Ohio, did not make the haul.

The reason given was work.

The reason given was work the previous time as well.

The Word of Mr. Thomas Kavanagh

I must be precise here, because the precision is the whole business.

When the question was first put to him — would he come north — Mr. Thomas Kavanagh committed on the instant. No hedge. No hesitation. No calendar consulted. No apparent regard whatsoever for the six hundred and forty miles standing between the promise and the keeping of it. It was, by every account the Society has gathered, a magnificent piece of theatre.

He did not come.

Both of those sentences describe the same gentleman, and I am told the Great Ledger has room for both. Mr. Kavanagh owns his own painting concern. He answers to no employer. He is, on paper, the freest man on this Society's roll of aspirants, and he has now converted that freedom into two consecutive absences, which is a return on liberty that this reporter finds genuinely remarkable.

The Vindication of Mr. John Kavanagh

His brother John watched Thomas make that pledge and did not believe a word of it. He said so, plainly, to his brother's face, and has gone on saying so since — that it was smoke, that no such promise survives contact with a Saturday.

Mr. John Kavanagh was right.

He now enjoys the rare and glorious distinction of having called his brother's bluff in front of witnesses and been vindicated by events. I would be delighted for him, and I would be delighted for him at considerable length, were it not for the one detail attached to his triumph.

He did not come either.

I have turned this over for some time. Mr. John Kavanagh predicted, accurately, that a Kavanagh would fail to appear at the Great Northern Open. He simply appears not to have realised the prophecy was load-bearing in both directions. The Society has recorded the prediction as correct. The Society has recorded the predictor as absent. Both entries stand.

Their Father Has Now Intervened

It has come to this.

Mr. Paul Kavanagh — father to Thomas and John, and himself an aspirant of this Society — has been obliged to involve himself in the golfing arrangements of two grown men, both of whom own vehicles and one of whom owns a company.

He looked at the arithmetic that had defeated his sons twice and proposed the remedy that had occurred to nobody in six years: if the Ohio men will not come to Wisconsin, let Wisconsin go to Ohio.

I would like the reader to understand what an extraordinary proposal that is. This Society does not travel. It has never travelled. Its entire competitive calendar sits within an easy drive of the crossroads where the thing was founded, and the standing position on out-of-state aspirants has always been that the road is theirs to cover.

That remains the Society's position. The Society has simply exhausted its appetite for being right about it while the first tee stands empty.

The Outing

Saturday, the eighteenth of October. The Commodore travels, and a delegation of the membership travels with him. Eighteen holes on Ohio ground, played as guests — a posture this membership has had almost no occasion to practise and which I expect it to handle poorly.

The grounds are not yet published. Neither are the tee times. I asked. I was told the particulars rest with the Committee, which is where particulars rest, and that they shall be entered into the Great Ledger in due course. I have printed below what is known and left blank what is not, which the Chief Secretary assures me is correct practice and which I have decided to be proud of.

What I can report is that the third week of October does something to a fairway in the late afternoon — the light comes in low and long and the whole course goes the colour of a good bourbon for about forty minutes before it quits on you entirely. You get that twice a year if you are fortunate. I am told Ohio gets it too. I intend to verify this personally and at length.

And the Jackets?

Not mine to say, and I have been reminded of that.

The question of whether jackets are bestowed upon the eighteenth has been raised, is understood to be live, and has gone where such questions go. The Council of Elders is understood to have convened upon the matter. The particulars of that convening are not understood — not by me, not by anyone I have thought to ask — and I have lately stopped thinking to ask.

What I will set down is this. Three Kavanaghs now stand upon the roll of aspirants: Thomas, John, and their father, which so far as the Archivist can determine has no precedent in the Society's records. A delegation is driving to Ohio. Jackets have been mentioned. I am not permitted to draw a conclusion from those three facts and I am not going to draw one.

I merely observe that all three are true at once, and that the two gentlemen at the centre of it will, for the first time in six years, have no drive to blame.

We are coming to you, gentlemen. Be outside.

I am fine, incidentally. It has been a long afternoon and the room is warm, and I have been asked to stop saying “six hundred and forty miles” in the office.

✦ A Proclamation From the Commodore

For six years this Society has held that the road belongs to the aspirant. I have defended that position with vigour, at length, and — it now emerges — to an empty first tee on two separate occasions.

Mr. Paul Kavanagh has proposed that we drive to Ohio ourselves. I have accepted. This should not be mistaken for agreement.

Let the record show that the road still belongs to the aspirant. We are simply meeting him partway, by which I mean the entire way, at our own expense, on a Saturday.

Three Kavanaghs now stand upon our roll — a father and his two sons, which has never once happened here. I note without further comment that it required the father to arrange it.

As to jackets: not my ruling to make, and I have been reminded of it with some warmth. The matter sits where such matters sit. I shall bring the garment bag regardless, and I shall make no promises whatever about what is inside it.

Gentlemen — we shall see you on the eighteenth. All of us. That is rather the point.

Peter Gelhar Commodore · Pop Whiskey Society of Wisconsin
